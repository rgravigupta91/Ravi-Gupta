Are We Underutilizing AI?



A few days ago, I asked my three-year-old daughter a very simple question.


Me: "What will you become when you grow up?"
She thought for a moment and said,
She: "I want to be a gorilla."

I laughed. Of all the things she could have said, she wanted to be a gorilla.
But later, I kept thinking about it. Why a gorilla? And then I remembered something that had happened a few days earlier. One of the kids in her daycare had been pretending to be a tiger. He would pretend to bite the other kids. I know the kid and his parents, so I knew he was just playing. Nothing serious. My daughter had mentioned it to me, and I casually told her, "You become a gorilla. Gorillas are big. A tiger can't eat a gorilla." That's it. I didn't think much about it at the time. But perhaps she did.

A few days later, when I asked her what she wanted to become, she remembered the solution she had been given. Gorilla. And suddenly, her answer made complete sense. She wasn't really thinking about what she wanted to be when she was 25. But she was solving a problem, her current problem. She had a problem — what if a tiger tries to bite me? And she had a tool — become a gorilla.

That got me thinking about something completely different. Are we doing the same thing with AI?
We now have tools that can:

  • write code.
  • write test cases.
  • read Jira tickets.
  • create documentation.
  • Summarize Meetings
  • Write Emails
  • Can analyze data
  • They can even build software with surprisingly little human intervention.

And naturally, that's where a lot of our attention has gone.

"How can AI make my existing work faster?" And that's a perfectly reasonable question. If AI can take something that normally takes me two hours and do it in ten minutes, that's valuable.

But then I started wondering... Is that really the best we can do with AI? Maybe we are doing exactly what my daughter did. Solved her current problem with a tool she had available. She didn't thisnk about the possibility of becoming a lion or a bear. She didn't think about the possibility of becoming something completely different. She solved her problem with the tool she had."

We have been given a new, incredibly powerful tool. And instead of asking what new things this tool makes possible, we are using it to solve the problems we already had. We are making the tiger problem easier. But perhaps we should be asking a different question. 😊

What problems can we solve now that we couldn't solve before? That's a very different question. Traditional software is incredibly powerful. We have spent decades building systems, databases, APIs, automation, distributed architectures and all kinds of tools to make computers do what we tell them to do. But there were always certain boundaries. Computers needed structured instructions. Data often needed to be structured. Someone had to explicitly define the rules. Someone had to anticipate the possible scenarios.

AI changes some of those boundaries. For the first time, software can work with things that were previously difficult to automate — language, images, ambiguous information, patterns, context and, to some extent, reasoning. That doesn't just mean we can build the same software faster. It potentially means we can build different kinds of software. And that's the part I find interesting.

What happens when we stop asking: "How can AI automate my existing work?"

and start asking: "What becomes possible because AI exists?"

Maybe there are problems in healthcare, education, science, climate, accessibility, research, finance, software engineering and countless other areas that were simply too expensive, too complex or too dependent on human effort to solve at scale. Maybe AI can help us approach some of them differently. I don't have a grand answer to this. In fact, that's partly why I am writing this. I want to explore it. I want to look at things people are already building with AI — not just another chatbot or another code-generation tool, but systems where AI is actually enabling something that would have been extremely difficult, impractical, or perhaps impossible with conventional software engineering.

And maybe there is something we can learn from those examples. Not necessarily the technology. Not necessarily the architecture. But the way of thinking. Because perhaps the biggest opportunity with AI isn't making us better at doing the things we already do. Perhaps it's giving us the ability to imagine and build things we previously couldn't. And maybe that's the question worth asking. If a three-year-old can look at the tools available to her and find a completely different way to solve a problem...

why shouldn't we do the same with AI?
