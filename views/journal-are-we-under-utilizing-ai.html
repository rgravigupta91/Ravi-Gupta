Are We Under-Utilizing AI?

A question worth asking.

A few days ago, I asked my three-year-old daughter a very simple question.

Me: "What will you become when you grow up?"

She thought for a moment and said —

"I want to be a gorilla."

I laughed. Of all the things she could have said, she wanted to be a gorilla. But later, I kept thinking about it. Why a gorilla?

And then I remembered something that had happened a few days earlier. One of the kids in her daycare had been pretending to be a tiger. He would pretend to bite the other kids. I know the kid and his parents, so I knew he was just playing. Nothing serious.

My daughter had mentioned it to me, and I casually told her: "You become a gorilla. Gorillas are big. A tiger can't eat a gorilla." That's it. I didn't think much about it at the time. But perhaps she did.

A few days later, when I asked her what she wanted to become, she remembered the solution she had been given. Gorilla.

And suddenly, her answer made complete sense. She wasn't really thinking about what she wanted to be when she was 25. She was solving a problem — her current problem.

  • She had a problem — what if a tiger tries to bite me?
  • She had a tool — become a gorilla.

Are we doing the same thing with AI?

We now have tools that can write code, write test cases, read Jira tickets, create documentation, summarize meetings, write emails, analyze data — and even build software with surprisingly little human intervention.

And naturally, that's where a lot of our attention has gone.

"How can AI make my existing work faster?"

That's a perfectly reasonable question. If AI can take something that normally takes two hours and do it in ten minutes, that's valuable.

But then I started wondering — is that really the best we can do with AI?

Maybe we are doing exactly what my daughter did. Solving the current problem with the tool we have available. She didn't think about becoming a lion or a bear. She didn't imagine something completely different. She solved her problem with the tool she had.

We have been given a new, incredibly powerful tool. And instead of asking what new things this tool makes possible, we are using it to solve the problems we already had. We are making the tiger problem easier.

But perhaps we should be asking a different question.

Instead of asking: "How can AI automate my existing work?"

What if we asked: "What becomes possible because AI exists?"

That's a very different question.

Traditional software is incredibly powerful. We spent decades building systems, databases, APIs, automation, and distributed architectures to make computers do what we tell them to do. But there were always certain boundaries. Computers needed structured instructions. Data often needed to be structured. Someone had to explicitly define the rules and anticipate every scenario.

AI changes some of those boundaries. For the first time, software can work with things that were previously difficult to automate — language, images, ambiguous information, patterns, context and, to some extent, reasoning.

That doesn't just mean we can build the same software faster. It potentially means we can build different kinds of software.

A Different Way of Thinking About Software

In conventional software development, we define everything upfront. We are given the input, we write the rules, and the system produces the output. The logic is explicit. Every decision is traceable. If something goes wrong, you can follow the code and find exactly where.

CONVENTIONAL SOFTWARE

Input + Rules → Output

⇄

AI / MACHINE LEARNING

Input + Output → Rules

In AI, we flip that. We provide massive amounts of input and output — examples, labels, outcomes — and the system discovers the rules on its own. Rules that are often so complex, so deeply embedded in patterns across millions of data points, that they cannot be written down. Cannot be explained. Sometimes cannot even be understood by the humans who built the system.

That is not a limitation. That is the capability.

Because it means AI can solve problems where the rules were never knowable in the first place.

What This Makes Possible

Let me give two examples that I find genuinely remarkable — not because AI made something faster, but because AI made something possible.

Detecting Blindness Before It Happens

Google — Diabetic Retinopathy Detection

Diabetic retinopathy is a condition where diabetes gradually damages the blood vessels in the retina. Caught early, it is treatable. Left undetected, it leads to blindness. The problem is that the early signs are subtle — small changes in the pattern of blood vessels that even experienced ophthalmologists can miss.

Google trained an AI on hundreds of thousands of retinal photographs, each labeled by expert doctors. The model learned to detect retinopathy with accuracy that matched — and in some cases exceeded — human specialists.

But here is what makes this remarkable. No one wrote the rules. No doctor sat down and described, in code, what early retinopathy looks like at the pixel level. The pattern was discovered — learned from the data — and it is not fully explainable even today.

This AI is now running in clinics in rural India where there are no ophthalmologists. Patients who would have gone undiagnosed until they lost their sight are being screened accurately. The question AI answered was not "how do we make ophthalmologists faster?" — it was "how do we give everyone access to ophthalmologist-level care?"

Solving a 50-Year-Old Problem in Biology

DeepMind — AlphaFold

Every living cell runs on proteins. Proteins are chains of amino acids that fold into specific three-dimensional shapes — and that shape determines what the protein does. Understanding the shape is fundamental to understanding disease, designing drugs, and developing treatments.

For over 50 years, predicting how a protein would fold from its amino acid sequence was one of biology's hardest unsolved problems. Experimentally determining a single protein structure could take years of lab work and cost millions of dollars. The rules governing folding are governed by physics at a scale so complex that no human could write them explicitly.

In 2020, DeepMind's AlphaFold solved it. Trained on the known structures of hundreds of thousands of proteins, it discovered the patterns — patterns that decades of human science had failed to formalize — and can now predict a protein's structure in minutes with remarkable accuracy.

AlphaFold has since released predictions for over 200 million proteins — nearly every known protein on Earth. Researchers are now designing medicines for diseases that previously had no viable path forward. This did not speed up biology. It opened a door that was closed.

Two very different fields. Two very different problems. But the same underlying shift — from software that executes rules we define, to systems that discover rules we never could.

Maybe there are problems in healthcare, education, science, climate, accessibility, research, finance, and software engineering that were simply too expensive, too complex, or too dependent on human effort to solve at scale. Maybe AI can help us approach some of them differently.

Why I'm writing this

I don't have a grand answer. In fact, that's partly why I am writing this — I want to explore it.

I want to look at things people are already building with AI — not just another chatbot or another code-generation tool, but systems where AI is actually enabling something that would have been extremely difficult, impractical, or perhaps impossible with conventional software engineering.

And maybe there is something we can learn from those examples. Not necessarily the technology. Not necessarily the architecture. But the way of thinking.

Because perhaps the biggest opportunity with AI isn't making us better at doing the things we already do. Perhaps it's giving us the ability to imagine and build things we previously couldn't.

My daughter solved her problem with the tool she had. And for a three-year-old, that was exactly the right thing to do. It was practical. It was clever. It worked.

But we are not three years old. We have spent years inside industries, systems, and problems that most people never get close to. We understand what is broken, what is slow, what is impossible — and why.

That visibility is rare. And AI, for the first time, gives us a tool that can match the scale of those problems.

So maybe the question worth asking is not just — how do I use this tool to work faster.

But — what have I seen, in my years of work, that was always too hard to fix? What problem did I quietly accept as unsolvable?

Because that might be exactly where AI belongs.
