LLM Is the Heart. But Is It the Only One?

On SLMs, fine-tuning, and choosing the right model for the right problem.

Take any modern AI application — a customer support bot, a document summarizer, a code assistant, a RAG pipeline, an agentic system — and look at what sits at the centre of it.

In almost every case, you will find a Large Language Model.

The LLM reasons. It reads context. It generates responses. It decides which tool to call next. It interprets retrieved documents. It writes the final answer. Remove the LLM, and the entire system stops. It is not one component among many. It is the heart.

But a question has been sitting quietly behind all of this progress:

Does the heart have to be this large? And does it always have to be someone else's?

The Real Cost of Defaulting to LLMs

Large Language Models are remarkable. But remarkable does not mean appropriate for every situation. There are real costs to using them, and those costs are easy to underestimate when you are excited about what they can do.

Cost Per Call

Every API call to a frontier LLM costs money. At low volumes, it is trivial. At production scale — millions of calls per day — it becomes significant.

Latency

Large models are slow. For applications where response time matters — real-time assistants, high-frequency decisions — latency is a hard constraint.

Privacy

Sending data to an external API means your data leaves your environment. For sensitive industries — healthcare, finance, legal — this is often not acceptable.

Overkill

Sometimes the task is narrow. Classify this email. Detect this anomaly. Extract this field. A trillion-parameter model is not the right tool for a three-category classification problem.

The question is not whether LLMs are powerful. They are. The question is whether they are always the right choice. And the answer, increasingly, is no.

Small Language Models — The Case for Smaller

A Small Language Model is exactly what it sounds like — a language model with significantly fewer parameters than a frontier LLM. Models like Microsoft's Phi, Meta's Llama, Google's Gemma, and Mistral can run on a laptop. Some can run on a mobile device.

For many tasks, they perform surprisingly well. And what they lack in raw capability, they often make up for in speed, cost, and the ability to run entirely within your own infrastructure.

WHEN AN SLM MAKES MORE SENSE THAN AN LLM

  • The task is well-defined and does not require broad general knowledge
  • Data privacy prevents sending information to external APIs
  • Response time is critical and cannot tolerate network latency
  • The application runs on edge devices or in constrained environments
  • Cost at scale makes frontier LLM APIs economically unviable

Fine-Tuning — Teaching a Model Your Language

A pre-trained model — whether large or small — knows a lot about the world in general. But it may know very little about your specific domain. A general LLM knows what a contract is. It does not know the specific terminology, clauses, and patterns in your company's contracts. A general model knows what SAP is. It does not know your custom configurations, your internal processes, or your specific transaction flows.

Fine-tuning is the process of taking a pre-trained model and continuing to train it on your domain-specific data. You are not training from scratch. You are starting from a model that already understands language, and teaching it to understand your language.

The result is a model that speaks your domain fluently — often outperforming a much larger general model on your specific tasks, at a fraction of the inference cost.

Task-Specific Models — When You Don't Need Language At All

Not every AI problem is a language problem. And not every problem that involves text needs an LLM.

If you want to classify customer support tickets into five categories, a fine-tuned BERT model — a fraction of the size of a modern LLM — will likely outperform a frontier LLM while being 100x cheaper and 10x faster.

If you want to detect anomalies in transaction data, a traditional machine learning model trained on your historical data will outperform anything a general LLM can do — because it was trained specifically on your patterns, not general human knowledge.

The instinct to reach for an LLM is understandable. They are impressive and versatile. But versatility is not the same as optimality.

Choosing the Right Model

Situation Right Approach
Broad reasoning, general knowledge, complex language tasks Frontier LLM
Domain-specific language tasks with private data Fine-tuned LLM or SLM
Real-time, low-latency, or on-device applications SLM
Narrow classification or extraction tasks Fine-tuned small model
Structured data — anomaly detection, forecasting, patterns Traditional ML model

The most expensive mistake I see in AI projects is not a technical one. It is defaulting to the most powerful, most general, most expensive option because it feels like the safest choice. It rarely is.

A frontier LLM is the right tool when the problem genuinely requires broad knowledge, flexible reasoning, and language understanding at scale. For everything else, there is usually a better fit — something smaller, faster, cheaper, and more precise.

The LLM is the heart of the modern AI stack. That is not going to change.

But a good engineer does not pick the most powerful component. They pick the right one.

Understanding when to use a frontier LLM, when to fine-tune, when to reach for an SLM, and when to skip language models entirely — that is the skill that will separate good AI engineering from expensive AI experimentation.
