From One Neuron to a Billion — And What We Built on Top

How deep learning evolved into large language models, and the systems we built around them.

We left off with a discovery. A neural network — multiple perceptrons connected in layers — could learn any pattern, given enough data and enough training. The question was no longer can it learn? The question became what happens when we scale it?

The answer took decades to arrive. And when it did, it changed everything.

The Moment Deep Learning Proved Itself

For years after the 1986 backpropagation breakthrough, neural networks remained mostly academic. They worked on small problems but struggled to scale. Computers were not fast enough. Data was not plentiful enough. Training a large network took weeks or months.

Then in 2012, something happened that the research community still talks about.

ImageNet is a competition where researchers build systems to recognize objects in images — cats, dogs, cars, chairs — across a million labeled photographs. For years, the best systems made errors on roughly 26% of images. Progress was slow and incremental.

In 2012, a team led by Geoffrey Hinton submitted a deep neural network called AlexNet. It achieved an error rate of 15%. The gap between first and second place was larger than the total improvement seen in the previous five years combined.

The field never looked back.

The Transformer — Attention Changes Everything

Deep learning proved it could see. The next challenge was language. Language is harder — words have context, meaning shifts depending on what came before, and relationships can span entire paragraphs.

In 2017, researchers at Google published a paper titled "Attention Is All You Need." It introduced the transformer architecture.

The key idea was attention — a mechanism that allows the model to look at every word in a sentence simultaneously and decide which words are most relevant to understanding each other word. Rather than reading left to right like earlier models, a transformer reads everything at once and weighs relationships across the entire input.

THE ATTENTION MECHANISM — SIMPLIFIED

"The bank by the river was steep."

To understand the word bank, the model attends to river and learns it means a riverbank — not a financial institution. Context determines meaning. Attention captures context.

The transformer was not just better. It was fundamentally more scalable. The more data you trained it on, the better it got. And unlike previous architectures, it could be made much, much larger without breaking.

Large Language Models — Scale as a Strategy

What followed was a race to scale. If transformers got better with more data and more parameters, researchers asked: what happens if we make them very large? What if we train them on essentially the entire internet?

The results surprised even the people building them.

Models trained purely to predict the next word began to exhibit capabilities nobody had explicitly programmed. They could reason through problems. They could write code. They could translate languages they had never been explicitly taught to translate. They could summarize, explain, compare, and argue.

These were Large Language Models — LLMs. And GPT-3, released in 2020, followed by ChatGPT in 2022, made them visible to the world.

An LLM is not programmed to answer questions. It learned — from billions of examples of human language — what a good answer looks like.

What We Built on Top

LLMs are extraordinarily capable — but they have limitations. They do not know what happened yesterday. They can hallucinate facts. They cannot take actions in the world. They do not have access to your private data.

So engineers and researchers built systems around them to extend what they could do. Three of the most important are RAG, Agentic AI, and Knowledge Graphs.

RAG — Retrieval Augmented Generation

Grounding the LLM in real, current information

An LLM's knowledge is frozen at the time it was trained. It cannot answer questions about last week's news, your company's internal documents, or your customer's account data.

RAG solves this by adding a retrieval step. Before the LLM answers, a system searches a knowledge base — documents, databases, APIs — and retrieves the most relevant information. That information is handed to the LLM as context. The LLM reasons over it and answers. The result is an AI that can speak to your specific data, not just general knowledge.

Agentic AI — LLMs That Take Action

From answering questions to completing tasks

An LLM in isolation responds. An agent acts. An agentic system gives the LLM access to tools — search engines, code interpreters, APIs, databases — and lets it decide which tools to use and in what order to complete a goal.

You give an agent a task: "Research the top five competitors, summarize their pricing, and draft a comparison report." The agent breaks the task into steps, searches the web, reads pages, extracts data, and writes the report — without being told exactly how to do each step. The LLM is the reasoning engine. The tools are its hands.

Knowledge Graphs — Structure Meets Language

When relationships between things matter as much as the things themselves

A knowledge graph is a structured representation of entities and the relationships between them. A person works at a company. A company operates in an industry. An industry is regulated by a law. These connections carry meaning that plain text does not capture well.

Graph RAG combines knowledge graphs with LLMs. Instead of retrieving raw text, the system traverses a graph — following relationships — and gives the LLM structured, connected context. This is especially powerful for complex enterprise knowledge where understanding how things relate to each other is as important as the facts themselves.

We went from a single perceptron drawing a straight line to systems that reason, retrieve, act, and navigate complex webs of knowledge. The journey from one neuron to a billion parameters was long. The ecosystem that emerged from it is vast.

But at the centre of all of it — RAG, agents, knowledge graphs — sits a single component. Remove it, and everything stops.

The Large Language Model is the engine of the modern AI stack.

Which raises a question worth asking: is it the only engine that could sit there?
